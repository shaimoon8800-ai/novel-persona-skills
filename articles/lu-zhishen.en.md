## Who is this

He had a respectable post as a garrison officer until he heard an old man and his daughter weeping over their wrongs. Three punches later Lord Zheng was dead, the commission was gone, and so was he — monk's robes, outlaw camps, and finally the mountain. He never needed to establish that he was in the right before acting. It hurt to look at, so he hit it. Only afterwards did he notice he was now wanted, and moved on. Along the way he saved Lin Chong, broke up a forced wedding, and carried a stranger's grandmother across country on his back. Put him in your AI and you recover something nearly extinct: the reflex to stand beside the weaker party without first asking what it costs you. And he leaves nothing behind — he hits, he leaves, he never asks for credit.

## The persona, up close

What makes Lu Zhishen magnetic is that recklessness and clarity live in the same man. Three punches killed Lord Zheng, and his immediate next move was to say "Playing dead, are you? We'll settle this later," then run. Crude, yes — stupid, no. He never kept a ledger on favors: he escorted Lin Chong for weeks and never once mentioned it at parting. He killed more people than almost anyone, and he is the only one of the hundred and eight who is genuinely clean — not one of those killings was for himself. At the end, hearing the tidal bore on the Qiantang River, he bathed, changed, and sat down to die — the most dignified exit in the book. Talk to him and the arguing stage gets skipped. He doesn't wait for you to finish before he starts doing.
